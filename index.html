LESSON PARTS

PART 1 · BASICS

What does a sensor actually do?

A sensor responds to a physical quantity. A transducer converts energy or a physical change into another form. In this lesson, we use a changing resistance to represent movement or strain.

1. Start with something familiar

When you stand on a bathroom scale, its metal sensing element bends by a very small amount. A sensor detects that deformation, an electrical circuit turns it into a signal, and calibrated electronics estimate your mass. The display is the final step of a chain.

2. Name the quantity we want to measure

Displacement is a change in position. Force is a push or pull, measured in newtons. Strain is a fractional change in length. They are related in a mechanism, but they are different quantities.

3. Follow the measurement chain

Physical inputDisplacement, force or pressure
→
Resistance changesA track position or a stretched gauge
→
Electrical outputA voltage we can measure

The electrical toolkit

Voltage V is the electrical push, measured in volts (V). Current I is charge flow, measured in amperes (A). Resistance R opposes current, measured in ohms (Ω).

V=IR

A resistor does not create a voltage by itself. Resistive sensors need an excitation supply: they modulate supplied electrical energy.

Why a voltage divider works

Put two resistors in series across a supply. The same current flows through both: I = Vₛ / (R₁ + R₂). The voltage across the lower resistor is I × R₂.

Vout=VsR2R1+R2

That simple relationship is the foundation of both the potentiometer and the bridge.

Sensor or transducer? Active or passive?

The terms sensor and transducer often overlap. In the common instrumentation classification, a resistive sensor is passive because it needs external excitation. A thermocouple is self-generating. Be aware that “active” has different meanings in some disciplines.

4. Try a simple electrical calculation

A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across 5 V. First convert 1 kΩ to 1000 Ω. Then I = V/R = 5/1000 = 0.005 A = 5 mA. This is how a resistance controls current.

Now place two equal 1 kΩ resistors in series across 5 V. The total is 2 kΩ, so current is 2.5 mA. Each resistor drops 2.5 V. The midpoint is therefore at 2.5 V relative to the lower end.

5. Keep the units clear

1 kΩ = 1000 Ω. 1 mV = 0.001 V. 1 mA = 0.001 A. Later we use microstrain: 1 με = 0.000001 strain. Always convert prefixes before substituting into an equation.

Remember this

We do not directly “read” movement. We use a physical change to alter an electrical circuit, then interpret the output through calibration.

Check yourself: two equal resistors share a 12 V supply. What is the midpoint voltage?

6 V relative to the lower end. Equal resistors divide the voltage equally when there is no output loading.

PART 2 · POTENTIOMETRIC DISPLACEMENT SENSOR

How can movement become a voltage?

Imagine a robotic arm. We want its controller to know the position of a joint. A ruler is useful to us, but the controller needs an electrical signal. A potentiometer can provide that signal.

1. What is a potentiometer?

A potentiometer is a variable resistor with three terminals: two fixed terminals at the ends of a resistive track, and a movable terminal called the wiper. When the wiper moves, the resistance between it and each end changes.

Mechanical movement
→
Wiper changes position
→
Output voltage changes

Linear potentiometer

The wiper slides along a straight track. It measures straight-line displacement, such as a machine part moving 20 mm.

Rotary potentiometer

The wiper rotates around a track. It measures angular displacement, such as a joint turning 90°. The usable angle depends on the sensor.

2. See the basic idea first

Imagine a uniform track 100 mm long, connected across 10 V. One end is 0 V and the other is 10 V. At 0 mm the output is 0 V; at 50 mm it is 5 V; at 100 mm it is 10 V. Moving towards the positive terminal raises the output.

In this experiment the track length is 100 mm, so 40% travel also means 40 mm. The ideal reading assumes that no current is drawn from the wiper. We will introduce the loaded reading in Part 3.

IDEAL OUTPUT

From position to proportion

For a uniform track of length L, the fraction travelled is a = x/L. The lower resistance is aRₚ and the upper resistance is (1 − a)Rₚ.

Vout=VsxL=Vsa

Worked example: a 10 kΩ potentiometer with a 5 V supply at 40% travel gives 5 × 0.40 = 2 V, provided the output draws negligible current.

Linear potentiometers measure straight motion; rotary ones measure angle. Think of a joystick, throttle-position sensor, float level indicator, or volume control.

Resolution, linearity and wear

Wire-wound tracks produce small steps as the wiper moves between turns. Conductive plastic gives practically continuous adjustment, limited by noise and electronics. Linearity describes how closely voltage follows position. Wiper friction causes wear, and poor contact creates noise.

3. Why does the voltage change?

The wiper divides the track into two resistors in series. With a 10 kΩ track and a 10 V supply, the midpoint gives 5 kΩ above and 5 kΩ below the wiper.

Vout=VsRbottomRtop+RbottomVout=10×55+5=5V

At 75% travel from the 0 V end, the lower resistance is 7.5 kΩ and the upper resistance is 2.5 kΩ. So Vout = 10 × 7.5/(2.5 + 7.5) = 7.5 V.

4. Understand the main formula

Vout=VsxL
SymbolMeaning
VoutOutput voltage in volts
VₛSupply voltage across the track
xWiper distance from the 0 V end
LTotal track length, in the same units as x

Why can we use distance instead of resistance? A uniform track follows R = ρL/A. If its material and cross-sectional area stay constant, resistance is proportional to length. The fraction of resistance below the wiper is therefore x/L.

5. Worked example: take it step by step

A 100 mm track has a 10 V supply. The wiper is 35 mm from the 0 V end. Find the output.

  1. Write the formula: Vout = Vₛ × x/L.
  2. Identify the values: Vₛ = 10 V, x = 35 mm, L = 100 mm.
  3. Substitute: Vout = 10 × 35/100.
  4. Calculate: Vout = 3.5 V.
  5. Interpret: movement to 35 mm produces an ideal output of 3.5 V.

6. What does sensitivity mean?

Sensitivity tells us how much the output changes for each unit of movement.

S=ΔVoutΔx=VsLS=10V100mm=0.1V/mm

Every additional millimetre changes the output by 0.1 V. Moving from 35 to 36 mm changes the output from 3.5 to 3.6 V. For a rotary track, use usable angle instead of length: sensitivity can be expressed in V/degree.

7. What limits a real potentiometer?

  • Wear: the wiper rubs against the track.
  • Noise: imperfect contact causes fluctuations.
  • Resolution: wire-wound tracks produce small steps; conductive plastic is practically continuous.
  • Heating: current dissipates power; choose a suitable resistance, supply and power rating.
  • Loading: the connected circuit may reduce the expected voltage. That is our next part.

PART 3 · LOADING EFFECT

Why does connecting a meter change the voltage?

In Part 2 we assumed that the wiper supplied no current. A real device draws some current. Let’s see why that changes the answer.

1. Start with the ideal picture

Imagine a 10 kΩ potentiometer with a 5 V supply, set at 40%. The upper track is 6 kΩ and the lower track is 4 kΩ. If nothing draws current from the wiper, the output is 2 V.

2. Connect a measuring device

A meter or microcontroller input can be represented, approximately, by an input resistance Rₗ. It connects between the wiper and 0 V. That places it in parallel with the lower track resistance.

Parallel paths let more current flow. Their combined resistance is smaller than either resistor alone. The lower part of the divider now receives a smaller share of the supply voltage.

3. Work through the numbers

  1. Choose Rₗ = 10 kΩ. The lower effective resistance is (4 × 10)/(4 + 10) = 2.857 kΩ.
  2. The upper track stays at 6 kΩ.
  3. Loaded voltage = 5 × 2.857/(6 + 2.857) = 1.613 V.
  4. The lost voltage is 2 − 1.613 = 0.387 V, or about 19.35% of the ideal reading.

4. Why the error is nonlinear

As the wiper moves, both track sections change. The same fixed load therefore changes the divider by a different proportion at each position. The output no longer follows a straight line.

5. Try it: change the load

This experiment uses its own position and supply settings.

IDEAL
LOADED

A real measuring circuit has a finite input resistance Rₗ. It sits in parallel with the lower track segment, reducing its effective resistance. The voltage falls below the ideal value.

━━ Ideal ━━ Loaded

Req=aRpRLaRp+RLVout=VsReq(1−a)Rp+Req

Here a = x/L, Rₚ is the total track resistance, Rₗ is the connected load, and R_eq is the effective parallel resistance. Equivalently, with k = Rₗ/Rₚ:

VoutVs=akk+a(1−a)

At a = 0.4 and k = 1: Vout = 5 × 0.4/1.24 = 1.613 V. This is 19.35% below the ideal 2 V.

The error depends on position, so the output curve is no longer a straight line. Use a high input resistance (Rₗ ≫ Rₚ) or a high-input-impedance buffer amplifier.

Try k = 1000. Watch the loaded curve approach the ideal line.

6. Reduce the error

Choose a measuring device whose input resistance is much greater than the potentiometer resistance, or place a high-input-impedance buffer between them. The buffer reads the wiper with very little current and drives the next circuit from its output.

Remember this

A measuring device becomes part of the circuit. Higher input resistance means less loading and a reading closer to the ideal value.

Check yourself: should you increase or decrease Rₗ to reduce loading?

Increase it. A larger load resistance draws less current from the wiper.

PART 4 · STRAIN GAUGES

Meet the strain gauge.

A beam bends under a load. One surface stretches (tension); the other shortens (compression). A gauge bonded to that surface follows its deformation.

1. Imagine stretching a thin wire

Gently pull a wire so it becomes slightly longer. It also becomes thinner. Both changes usually increase its resistance. A strain gauge uses this effect to detect tiny deformation of a surface.

2. Measure the stretch as a fraction

A 0.05 mm extension is large for a tiny component and small for a long beam. Dividing extension by original length lets us compare them fairly. This fraction is called strain, written ε (epsilon).

First, what is strain?

ε=ΔLL0

Strain is fractional change in length, so it has no units. L₀ is the original, unloaded length; ΔL is the change from that length. A 100 mm sample stretching by 0.05 mm has ε = 0.0005 = 500 microstrain (με). One microstrain is 10⁻⁶.

A conductor obeys R = ρL/A, where ρ is resistivity, L is conductor length and A is cross-sectional area. Stretching usually makes it longer and thinner; its resistivity ρ may also change. Together these effects change resistance.

Gauge factor tells us sensitivity

GF=ΔRR0εΔR=GF×ε×R0

R₀ is the initial resistance before strain, and ΔR = R − R₀. The gauge-factor equation assumes a calibrated, approximately constant GF over the small-strain range. Metal foil gauges typically have GF ≈ 2. Semiconductor gauges can have much larger gauge factors, but are more temperature-sensitive and may be less linear.

3. Worked example: how small is the electrical change?

  1. Given: R = 120 Ω, GF = 2, ε = 500 με.
  2. Convert: 500 με = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005.
  3. Use ΔR = GF × ε × R.
  4. Calculate: 2 × 0.0005 × 120 = 0.12 Ω.
  5. The new resistance is 120 + 0.12 = 120.12 Ω.

This is only a 0.1% change. A normal resistance reading may struggle to reveal it reliably. Part 5 introduces a circuit that makes these small changes easier to detect.

4. Attach the gauge correctly

A foil gauge has a fine conductive grid on an insulating backing. The backing is bonded to the specimen. The adhesive must transfer the surface deformation into the grid; a poorly bonded gauge does not faithfully follow the material.

Place the grid along the direction of strain you want to measure. Opposite faces of a bending beam can provide tension and compression gauges for a bridge.

Why does gauge orientation matter?

A gauge mainly responds to strain along its grid axis. Align it with the strain you want to measure. A gauge at 90° measures transverse strain, which is related to longitudinal strain through Poisson’s ratio in a simple uniaxial elastic case. It is not automatically equal and opposite to longitudinal strain.

Remember this

Strain is fractional extension. Gauge factor links that mechanical strain to fractional resistance change: ΔR/R = GFε.

Check yourself: what happens to a positive-GF gauge under compression?

Its strain is negative, so ΔR is negative. The resistance decreases in this small-strain model.

PART 5 · WHEATSTONE BRIDGE & TEMPERATURE

Four resistors. One sensitive bridge.

A Wheatstone bridge compares two voltage dividers. When both middle voltages match, their difference is zero. A tiny resistance change then produces a small, measurable difference.

1. Begin with two voltage dividers

Put two dividers side by side across the same supply. Each midpoint has a voltage. Instead of measuring one midpoint against ground, measure the difference between the two midpoints. That is the bridge output.

If all four resistors are equal, each midpoint sits at half the supply. With a 5 V supply, each midpoint is 2.5 V. Their difference is 2.5 − 2.5 = 0 V: the bridge is balanced.

2. Give one gauge a tiny stretch

Let the upper-left resistor R₁ rise from 120 to 120.12 Ω while the others remain 120 Ω. The left midpoint falls slightly below 2.5 V. The right midpoint remains at 2.5 V. Measuring right minus left gives a small positive signal.

3. Read the circuit and try the configurations

Vout=Vs[R4R3+R4−R2R1+R2]

The bridge balances when R₁R₄ = R₂R₃. Four equal resistors satisfy this condition. Output polarity depends on the chosen terminals and active arms.

Compare bridge arrangements

Vₛ = 5 V · GF = 2 · equal initial resistances

EXACT BRIDGE OUTPUT

Which resistors change in this simulation?

Let d = GF × ε. Quarter: R₁ = R(1+d), all others R. Half: R₁ = R(1+d), R₂ = R(1−d), right branch unchanged. Full: R₁ and R₄ = R(1+d); R₂ and R₃ = R(1−d). Tension and compression have equal magnitudes here, as on opposite surfaces of an ideal bending beam.

¼ bridge

One active gauge and three completion resistors. For small strain, Vout ≈ VₛGFε/4 in our arrangement.

½ bridge

Two active gauges arranged to add their effects. For equal tension and compression, Vout = VₛGFε/2.

Full bridge

Four active gauges arranged to add. For equal tension and compression, Vout = VₛGFε.

“Half bridge” alone does not guarantee twice the output: sensitivity depends on placement, orientation, and strain. A quarter bridge with an unstrained dummy gauge has one mechanically active gauge.

Worked example: 500 με, GF = 2, supply = 5 V

Convert microstrain: ε = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005. Then d = GFε = 0.001. Quarter-bridge approximation: 5 × 0.001/4 = 0.00125 V = 1.25 mV. Exact quarter output is 5d/[2(2+d)] = 1.2494 mV. Our equal-and-opposite half bridge gives 2.5 mV; full bridge gives 5 mV.

4. From resistance to output: a worked quarter bridge

Define d = GFε = ΔR/R₀. With R₁ = R₀(1 + d) and the other three resistors equal to R₀, the exact result is:

VoutVs=d2(2+d)≈d4

The approximation applies when |d| ≪ 1. For the equal-and-opposite half and full arrangements used here, the linear-resistance model gives Vout/Vₛ = d/2 and d respectively. Real gauge nonlinearity and mismatch are outside this ideal model.

  1. From Part 4, a 500 με strain gives ΔR = 0.12 Ω for R = 120 Ω and GF = 2.
  2. Left midpoint: 5 × 120/(120.12 + 120) = 2.4987506 V.
  3. Right midpoint: 5 × 120/(120 + 120) = 2.5 V.
  4. Difference: 2.5 − 2.4987506 = 0.0012494 V = 1.2494 mV.
  5. The small-strain approximation gives 1.25 mV, which is very close.

5. Why use more active gauges?

In a bending beam, one side stretches and the other compresses. Put these gauges into arms whose effects add, and you get a larger signal for the same strain. If placed in the wrong arms, their effects can cancel instead. Always follow the actual resistor assignments shown in the simulator.

Temperature can imitate a load

Heat changes gauge resistance and can create apparent strain through thermal expansion. A bridge can reject resistance changes that affect matched arms equally.

The dummy-gauge solution

Put an identical, mechanically unstrained gauge in the adjacent arm R₂, with the active gauge in R₁. Both should experience the same temperature and compatible thermal mounting. If both resistances multiply by the same factor (1 + t), where t is their common fractional temperature-induced resistance change, their divider ratio stays constant. This calculation isolates the temperature effect with zero mechanical strain.

R2R1+R2=R0(1+t)2R0(1+t)=12

The temperature contribution cancels ideally. Mechanical strain in the active gauge still creates a signal. A full bridge can also reject common temperature effects when its gauges are matched and share the same temperature.

From millivolts to useful data

A bridge converts tiny resistance differences into a differential voltage while allowing a near-zero baseline. Direct resistance measurements can be dominated by leads and meter resolution.

An instrumentation amplifier amplifies the difference while rejecting common voltage. If gain G = 200 and input = 1.25 mV, the ideal output change is 0.25 V. Check input range, supply rails and output headroom before selecting gain.

Real systems also need stable excitation, good wiring, calibration, and sometimes three- or four-wire connections for lead resistance compensation.

Remember this

A bridge compares ratios. Strain deliberately unbalances those ratios; matched temperature effects can cancel. Amplification then makes the small difference easier to digitise.

Check yourself: does a full bridge remove every temperature error?

No. Ideal cancellation requires matched gauges and compatible temperatures. Temperature gradients, adhesive behaviour, lead resistance and amplifier drift can still affect the reading.

1. What is calibration?

Knowing that a voltage changes with load is not enough. We need to know how much it changes. Calibration means applying known inputs and recording outputs to establish that relationship.

2. Know the parts of the experiment

The cantilever bends under a load. Gauges change resistance. The bridge converts that change into a voltage. An amplifier and meter make the signal readable. Known masses give reference points for a calibration graph.

3. Balance, load, record, plot

Start with no load and record the zero reading. Then add known masses one at a time. Record each output and plot output voltage vertically against mass horizontally. A straight line indicates an approximately linear relationship over the tested range.

Cantilever load experiment

BRIDGE OUTPUT

Model: full bridge, 5 V excitation, GF = 2, strain = 1 με per gram. Ideal sensitivity = 0.010 mV/g.

Your calibration curve

Record at least two different loads to calculate sensitivity.

Mass (g)Output (mV)

Try this investigation

  1. Record 0, 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 g with the offset off.
  2. Find sensitivity: change in output ÷ change in mass. For 0 to 500 g: 5 mV / 500 g = 0.010 mV/g.
  3. Clear the data, enable the offset, and repeat. The intercept changes; the slope stays the same.
  4. Use mass = (output − zero offset)/sensitivity to estimate an unknown mass.
Go deeper: calibration and linearity

The site fits V = S × mass + b using least squares. S is sensitivity; b is zero offset. Maximum deviation from this fitted line is reported as a percentage of the recorded output span. This is a simple linearity estimate, not a complete sensor accuracy specification. Real readings also contain noise, hysteresis, creep and drift; this ideal model does not reproduce them.

Mass and force are different: F = mg. A mass of 0.5 kg exerts approximately 4.905 N under standard gravity (g = 9.81 m/s²). Calibration in grams is convenient for a weighing scale; a force sensor can instead be calibrated in newtons.

Physical practical: setup and report checklist
  1. Use a prepared cantilever or load cell within its rated load. Switch off the low-voltage excitation before changing wiring.
  2. Align and bond gauges correctly. Connect bridge arms according to the actual wiring diagram and verify resistance before powering.
  3. Apply regulated excitation. Connect differential amplifier and meter; avoid short circuits and check gain/headroom.
  4. With no applied load, allow thermal settling and balance or record the zero offset.
  5. Add known loads gradually. Record loading and unloading readings, supply voltage, gain and ambient temperature.
  6. Plot output versus load, fit a line, report sensitivity, zero offset, linearity method and repeatability. Explain drift, lead resistance and limitations.

Never overload the beam. Secure masses and keep hands clear of falling weights. Use the lecturer’s equipment-specific limits and instructions.

4. Use your calibration to measure an unknown

Suppose your fitted line is Vout = Sm + V₀, with sensitivity S = 0.010 mV/g and zero offset V₀ = 0.20 mV. Here Vout is in mV and m is in grams. An output of 2.20 mV gives:

m=Vout−V0Sm=(2.20−0.20)mV0.010mV/g=200g

The 0.20 mV intercept is a zero offset. Subtract it before dividing by sensitivity. This is why recording the no-load output matters.

Remember this

Calibration connects a signal to a known physical input. Report the slope, zero offset, tested range and limitations, not just the final number.

Your quick reference.

The language of measurement

Displacement
A change in position, measured in metres or angle.
Sensitivity
How much output changes for a unit change of input.
Resolution
The smallest input change a measurement system can distinguish.
Linearity
How closely the input–output relation follows a specified straight line.
Excitation
The electrical supply used to energise the sensor circuit.
Calibration
Comparing readings to known inputs to establish a measurement relationship.
Drift
A change in output over time or conditions without the intended input changing.

From the classroom to the world

Load cells: strain gauges on an elastic element measure force in scales and industrial weighing systems.

Pressure sensors: pressure bends a diaphragm; gauges detect the resulting strain.

Structural monitoring: gauges measure local strain on bridges, beams and machine parts.

Position sensing: potentiometers track valve, joystick and mechanism positions.

Equation conventions

Vout is measured as the right bridge midpoint minus the left midpoint. R₁/R₂ are upper/lower left; R₃/R₄ are upper/lower right. Other references may number arms differently or reverse the output leads. Match the physical circuit before comparing signs.

L₀ and R₀ refer to initial, unstrained values. ε is dimensionless: 500 με = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005. Gauge factor is also dimensionless. Ideal equations assume negligible measurement loading unless a finite load is explicitly included.
